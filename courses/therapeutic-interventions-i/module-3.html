TOPIC 3.1

Tissue Mechanics: Concepts, Bone, Articular Cartilage, Aging

Objectives: understand stress-strain curves, viscoelastic properties, stress relaxation, and creep.

1. Types of Loading

LoadWhat it doesExample
CompressionPushes togetherJoint compression — the femur sitting on the tibia
TensionPulls apartTendons and ligaments — a high tensile load is the ACL tear mechanism
ShearItems slide across each otherThe patellofemoral joint — patella sliding across the femur
TorsionTwisting along an axis
BendingTension on one side, compression on the other

2. Mechanical Property Definitions

TermDefinition
StressForce generated by resisting deformation — force ÷ area
StrainRatio of deformed length to original length
Stiffness (Young's modulus)Ratio of stress to strain — the slope in the elastic region
Elastic regionMaterial returns to its original shape after the load is removed
Plastic regionMaterial stays deformed after the load is removed
Yield pointWhere behaviour transitions from elastic to plastic
Ultimate failure pointWhere the material breaks
EnergyArea under the stress-strain curve — can be stored or dissipated
🎙
Think of the yield point as the point of no return — it's the inflection point. And the ultimate failure point is the other one.
  • Reading the curve in tissue terms: first slack is taken up and the fibres straighten. Then the elastic region — safe, reversible stretch, like an elastic band. Then the plastic region — permanent change. Then failure — the tissue breaks.
★
The reason any of this matters: this is exactly what happens when an athlete tears something. The deck uses Kevin Durant rupturing his Achilles in the NBA Finals as the picture of ultimate failure.

3. Viscoelasticity, Creep and Stress Relaxation

TermDefinition
ViscoelasticBehaviour changes with the rate of loading — it is velocity dependent. Load it faster and it behaves differently
CreepUnder a constant load, the material deforms and reaches an equilibrium state. Rapid initial deformation, then slow deformation until equilibrium
Stress relaxationUnder a constant deformation, stress builds initially then slowly decreases to equilibrium. It distributes the stress across the tissue — protective
BiphasicContains components with different properties (fluid + solid, organic + inorganic)
AnisotropicMechanical properties differ between loading modes — behaves differently in tension than in compression
🎙
Creep over a lifetime: as someone ages, the vertebrae eventually shrink in height. That's a form of creep — which is why older people are shorter than they were when they were younger.
🎙
Stress relaxation, made physical: stand on a bed. Your foot sinks in until it stops sinking in, and you're not breaking the bed either — it's just supporting you. That's stress relaxation.
★
Dr. Awruch flags biphasic and anisotropic as nice-to-know rather than need-to-know. Creep, stress relaxation and rate-dependence are the three the handout lists as take-home.
🎙
One forward-looking point he makes: just because something hurts, it doesn't mean there is something structurally wrong. Not very relevant for this class — very relevant later.

TOPIC 3.2

Stages of Healing

Objectives: discuss the phases of tissue healing, and apply the phases to cases across different tissues.

1. Types of Injury

InjuryWhat it is
StrainMusculotendinous unit — a pulled muscle. The classic one
SprainLigamentous injury, grades I–III — the grade goes up as more of the ligament is damaged
DislocationLoss of anatomical relationship of the joint
SubluxationPartial dislocation — think of it as a short-term dislocation
Muscle/tendon rupture or tearPartial or complete
TendinopathyTendon injury affected by mechanical loading — tendinitis acutely, tendinosis when it has been going on longer (inflammation, or inflammation with scar tissue)
🎙
On complete tears: it's not actually what you think. Sometimes a full tear just means it went completely through — it doesn't mean it's completely torn off.
ConditionWhat it is
SynovitisInflammation of a synovial membrane — e.g. a tendon sheath that exists to reduce friction, which sometimes gets inflamed itself
HemarthrosisBleeding into the joint (*heme* = blood, *arthrosis* = of the joint)
GanglionTendon or joint capsule wall ballooning — the classic ganglion cyst on the back of the hand
BursitisInflammation of a bursa. Harder to see; often a diagnosis of exclusion on orthopaedic exam
ContusionBruising from trauma
Overuse / cumulative traumaRepetitive low loads resulting in inflammation and pain
🎙
Two places Dr. Awruch corrects his own slide. First, he'd retitle this from 'types of injuries' to just 'conditions' — not all of these happen via an injury. Second, on overuse he'd drop the word 'low': common sense says repetitive HIGH loads cause overuse too.

2. SINSS

LetterMeaningWhat it buys you
S — SeverityHow intense and impactful is this in the person's lifeDetermines the appropriate vigour and extent of the examination and the interventions
I — IrritabilityWhat aggravates it, what eases itSame — sets how much you can do
N — NatureThe body system in questionDirects the focus of the exam, the intervention, and whether referral is needed
S — StageAcute (<6 weeks) · subacute (6–12 weeks) · chronic (>12 weeks)Identifies the stage of healing, which guides the corresponding intervention
S — StabilityImproving, worsening, or not changingIdentification of influencing factors
🎙
On the timeframes: there are different definitions, but what he's seen most is under 6 weeks acute, 6–12 weeks subacute, over 12 weeks chronic — with some discrepancy in the literature.
★
The stability point is the one with real clinical teeth. If a patient improves from visit 2 to 3 and again from 3 to 4, they will likely keep improving. If they keep getting worse over a long course of care, that's your cue to ask why, and to consider referral.
🎙
Why stage matters so much: if you know there's a soft-tissue injury to a muscle, and you know the tissue healing timeline for muscle, you can accurately predict when it's safe to start isometric strengthening and then isotonic strengthening.

3. The Four Phases

PhaseWhat is happeningWhat you see on examDuration
InflammationVascular, cellular, chemical and immune processes; cells, chemical changes, immune cells and fibroblasts rush to the areaSwelling, redness, warmth (signs) and pain (symptom). Movement is painful; PROM may produce muscle guarding before end range4–6 days
Proliferation & repairNoxious and inflammatory chemicals removed; capillary growth; collagen and granulation tissue formDecreased swelling, inflammation resolving. Pain at end-range resistance with ROM, and weakness10 days – 6 weeks, longer in tissues with poor vascularity
Maturation / remodelingConnective tissue maturation, scar tissue contracture, conversion of Type III collagen, reorganisation with stressInflammation completely resolved. Pain only at end range of available motion, past resistance. Weakness can persist6–12 months, variable with severity and tissue
🎙
The postoperative version of the inflammatory phase: you get a patient after a rotator cuff repair, you go to do passive range of motion, and they say stop pulling, it's really painful. You might even feel some spasm — they're just uncomfortable moving into that new range.
★
Why the 6-week mark keeps appearing in protocols: in a lot of postoperative protocols strengthening doesn't start until six weeks, which is the end of the proliferation window. Some physicians add an extra week on top.
🎙
On the 6–12 month maturation window: on paper it might last 12 months, but watching a patient perform, they're likely significantly stronger by month six or eight. You might need until the end of the year for full gains.

4. PT Goals by Phase

PhaseGoalDoPrecautions / contraindications
InflammationMaximum protectionControl the effects of inflammation (pain) · prevent the adverse effects of rest · cautious progression of movement · protective and assistive devices as indicatedContraindicated: stretching and resistance exercise to inflamed tissue
ProliferationModerate protection / controlled motionScar mobilisation · promote healing · progress ROM with caution · progress exercise intensityIncreased inflammation, or pain lasting > 24 hours
MaturationMinimal protection / return to functionImprove scar quality · increase strength and endurance · function-specific exercise · progress to return to work or play for high demandNo inflammation, or pain > 4 hours, or pain requiring medication
🎙
A distinction worth writing down: in the inflammatory phase he would replace 'stretching' with 'range of motion'. Range of motion is typically fine — stretching, meaning actively trying to gain range, is not. And the great thing about the resistance-exercise contraindication is that if you tried, the patient would tell you it hurts anyway.
🎙
On working inside a protocol, in the proliferation phase: if the protocol says you may move the shoulder to 110°, those are your parameters — but that doesn't mean you have to go to it. Tell the patient we're allowed to take you to 100°, let me know if it hurts before we get there, and let me know if you want me to stop.

Individualise to the patient

  • These are general timeframes — health and lifestyle will change them
  • Setbacks happen. Communicate and go back to the drawing board
  • Understand your patient's factors; education is key
  • Not every patient comes from a physician — with direct access you are the first one, so know the timelines and communicate
  • Usually the patient will tell you when they feel ready for the next stage
🎙
The photo of the forest is there on purpose: in PT school information comes in pieces, and when you see a case it's easy to clutch onto one item or one test instead of stepping back. As you learn more you may find you're sure of less — and that's okay. See the forest through the trees.

TOPIC 3.3

Needs Analysis

Objectives: identify the impairment bucket, identify the necessary task demands, identify the treatment bucket, and apply to cases.

1. The Impairment Bucket

BucketTreatment
1. WeaknessStrengthening
2. MobilityMobilization
3. PainTreat the pain

Bucket 1 — Weakness

  • Two different presentations both land here: decreased strength and hypermobility. Both are treated with strengthening.
HISTORY — DECREASED STRENGTHHISTORY — HYPERMOBILITY
“I get really fatigued and tired”“I've been double jointed since I was a kid”
“It's hard to do” · “difficulty”“Clicking and popping” — e.g. a swimmer with shoulder symptoms
Time of day is NOT consistent — worse at the end of the day, or pain only at certain times“I've had dislocations in the past”
Exam findingWhat you may see
AROMMaybe decreased — they can't lift the joint because they're too weak
PROMCould be increased — they're hypermobile
MMT / myotomeDecreased strength
Functional movementsDecreased quality — squat, lunge, single-leg squat. Watch a single-leg squat and see the knee wobbling
Joint mobsNormal or hypermobile
  • Findings, either/or: decreased strength, OR increased ROM = hypermobile.

Bucket 2 — Mobility

  • History: “hard to move it” · “feels tight” · “feels stuck” · “I can't” — and crucially, the same story any time of day.
🎙
Why the time-of-day question separates the first two buckets: if something is tight, it's going to be tight consistently. Someone getting tired after being at work all day is a very different story, and that points toward decreased strength.
  • Exam: PROM decreased · joint mobilization hypomobile. Findings: hypomobility on joint mobs, decreased ROM, and sometimes a firm end feel.
  • The cause is either the joint being tight or soft tissue restriction — so the treatment is stretching or mobilization.

Bucket 3 — Pain

  • History: pain and/or fear — “it hurts when…” · “I worry that…” · “I don't want to make it worse, so I don't move.”
  • Exam: empty end feel on ROM — pain well before end range. On MMT, pain or fear limits effort. Finding: painful, and possibly afraid of the pain.

2. Necessary Task Demands

STRENGTH → STRENGTHENMOBILITY → MOBILIZE
Clinical principle: if it is NOT a mobility requirement, then by default it is a strength requirement. Strength is the default bucketSimple as this: how much ROM does the task require?
Everything here requires active muscle contraction: power · agility · motor control · endurance · coordination · balanceYou only need enough ROM to do the task. A sprinter needs no more range than sprinting requires; a marathoner needs enough for the gait they run with. That's it
Sprinter → power and endurance. Soccer player → agility. Powerlifter → power. Century-ride cyclist → enduranceIf a patient already has enough range for their task, they default into the strength bucket and come out of the mobility bucket — range is checked off, so the remaining limitation must be strength

3. Putting It Together

Impairment bucket + task demands = treatment bucket

  • Is ROM sufficient? Compared to normal, AND for the task needed
  • Is strength sufficient? Compared to normal, AND for the task needed
  • The task demands can outweigh the impairment bucket
🎙
Two examples of task demand outweighing the impairment: someone with 5/5 MMT does not necessarily have enough strength to be an Olympic weightlifter. And a competitive cyclist with 5/5 strength on testing still may not be able to ride a hundred miles — that task demands endurance, not just strength.

Quick-Reference Glossary — Topic 3.3

TermDefinition
Impairment bucketWeakness, mobility, or pain — what the exam actually found
Necessary task demandsWhat the person needs to be able to do; strength or mobility
Treatment bucketImpairment bucket + task demands

TOPIC 3.4

Intervention Decision Making — Clinical Reasoning 1–3

A three-part series. The stated starting point is “it depends” — and the point of the series is to turn that into a repeatable decision.

1. What Range of Motion Tells You (Part 1)

FindingWhat it meansSo…
AROM normal and pain-freeYou can rule that joint out as the pain generator — full range, normal end feel, no painAdd overpressure (effectively PROM) to confirm. Surrounding musculature can still be involved
AROM < PROMA muscle power impairment — power is impaired, which means weaknessStrengthening may be a great antidote
AROM limited, and PROM can't make up the differenceSome form of tightness — a mobility impairmentStretching or mobilization
  • If AROM is limited, there are three possibilities: weakness, tightness, or pain — plus a fourth factor, fear.
  • If PROM is limited or painful, it is one of three things: a joint mobility deficit, a tissue extensibility deficit, or pain. It is not weakness — passive motion doesn't involve muscle activation, because you are producing the movement.
🎙
On fear: if someone was hurt previously, the brain remembers that experience, similar to other forms of trauma. After a car accident a person may be fearful the next time they drive on the highway. That fear alone can limit movement.
★
The telehealth argument for why ROM is so valuable: you can't put hands on the patient or perform orthopaedic tests, so you're doing a detailed subjective history and observing range of motion — and you're still getting about 95% of the information you'd normally get in clinic.
🎙
The musculoskeletal screen and the neuro screen are performed together. They're taught in isolation in a neuro class and a musculoskeletal class, but they are not performed in isolation — nerves activate and control muscles, and any pain felt is regulated by the nervous system.

2. Matching Finding to Intervention (Part 2)

FindingPairs with
WeaknessStrengthening
PainPain-focused treatment
FearPsychologically informed physical therapy
Joint mobility deficitJoint mobilization or manipulation
Tissue extensibility deficitStretching
🎙
Tightness has exactly two sources. Tissue extensibility deficits is a bucket term for anything soft-tissue — muscle, ligament, nerve, scar tissue. Joint mobility deficits mean the joint itself lacks mobility.

The ankle worked example — why precision matters

  • Limited dorsiflexion could be tissue extensibility (soleus, posterior tibialis, anything behind the ankle) or a joint mobility deficit (talocrural joint)
  • If the soleus is what's limiting it, mobilising the talocrural joint doesn't address the problem
  • If the talocrural joint is tight and the soleus and gastroc aren't, mobilising the joint is exactly right
  • It isn't dangerous to mobilise something that isn't tight — it's just unnecessary. You're using the wrong tool
🎙
The screwdriver analogy he keeps coming back to: if you needed a Phillips head but were given a flat head, you simply would not be able to target what you needed to target.
  • Weakness has two flavours too. Muscle-related — quadriceps testing 3+/5, so a long arc quad targets the quadriceps in that same position. Or motor control — a single-leg squat showing knee and hip adduction, valgus collapse, which is a combination of strength deficits, motor control deficits and likely hip abduction weakness. You strengthen those areas AND retrain the movement.
🎙
On pain being inhibitory: if you've ever stepped on a pebble barefoot, you probably noticed your knee buckle immediately. Pain causes rapid inhibition — it essentially short-circuits the quadriceps and the muscles needed to load the limb. That's why your leg may feel weaker the day after a knee injury even though nothing is torn.
★
Do not treat what you do not see. A hypermobile gymnast with low back pain does not need a flexibility exercise — if nothing is tight, what exactly are you stretching? Stretching cannot fix tightness that is not present.

3. The Interventions Themselves (Part 3)

InterventionFormsWhen
Joint mobilizationSelf-mobilization (e.g. foam roller for limited thoracic extension) · manual therapy mobilization · manipulationJoint mobility deficit
StretchingSelf-stretch · therapist-performed manual stretching · massage or instrument-assisted soft tissue mobilization (IASTM)Tissue extensibility deficit
Manual therapyDoes two things: increases ROM and reduces painTight joint, or a painful joint where the patient is having trouble relaxing
Psychologically informed careCBT · graded exposure · relaxation therapy · pain neuroscience education · activity pacingHigh pain, debilitating pain, fear avoidance, chronic pain
StrengtheningResistance training → motor control → power → running, cutting and return-to-sport drillsWeakness, and often indirectly as graded exposure
MOBILIZATIONMANIPULATION
Low velocity — slow, controlled force applied over timeHigh velocity, low amplitude
Gently applying force to get the segment moving and potentially reduce painVery fast, and the amplitude is quite low — possibly lower than a mobilization
Larger amplitude is possible because it's slowMove something quickly and it must be a very small movement. Large, fast movements are where things become unsafe
🎙
The CrossFit worked example for stretching: he struggles to keep his arms overhead at the bottom of an overhead squat. At the bottom the latissimus dorsi is wound up on both ends — it attaches to the pelvis and to the shoulder, so it's a bi-articular muscle. Home exercise, soft tissue work, IASTM, or a foam roller.

Activity pacing — breaking the boom-bust cycle

  • The pattern: overactivity → injury and pain → a lot of rest → return full steam → overactive again → pain again → rest even longer → weaker → repeat
  • Instead of the full activity that causes pain — running eight miles — you have them run two
  • Rest an appropriate amount, not long enough to become weaker, then return to the same activity
  • Gradually progress. This is how you move patients out of chronic pain
🎙
On why strengthening works when you might not expect it: knee pain often responds well to hip strengthening as well as hamstring and quadriceps strengthening. But when pain is the primary issue and pain levels are high, strengthening may not be realistic or appropriate initially.
  • Why the strengthening order matches protocols: look up an ACL reconstruction protocol and you'll see restore range of motion → strengthen → motor control → power → running → return to sport, all while respecting tissue healing timelines.

4. The Whole Framework, One Picture

  • Start at the body region in question. Assess AROM — full and pain-free rules the joint out. If limited or painful, gather more: assess PROM to separate weakness from tightness from pain or fear. PROM full and pain-free → strengthen. PROM limited or painful → address the limitation, or the pain, or the fear.
🎙
This can feel overwhelming, but slow down and recognise that you already know this information. All I have done is organise it into a systems-level framework that mirrors how clinicians think.
★
If it ever feels like too much, the whole series collapses to one rule: TREAT WHAT YOU SEE. Weakness → strengthening. Tightness → stretching or joint mobilization. Pain and fear → pain modulation and psychologically informed PT.

SYNC SESSION 3

Needs Analysis & Clinical Reasoning

The session opens with three questions to argue about: when would a patient benefit from joint mobilization vs stretching? How about strengthening? Manual therapy?

1. The Five-Step Method

The sequence you run on every case

  • 1. Identify the impairments
  • 2. Identify the needs of the task
  • 3. Identify the treatment bucket
  • 4. Pick a treatment
  • 5. Modify as needed — progress or regress

The exam findings, not the story, put them in different buckets.
