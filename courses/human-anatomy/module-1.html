TOPIC 1.1
Directional Terms

1. Body Planes

All description starts from anatomical position. Each plane pairs with the directional terms used within it:

Plane Divides the body... Directional terms
Sagittal Front to back (right/left portions); the mid-sagittal plane sits at the midline Medial (toward midline) vs lateral (away from midline)
Coronal / Frontal Into front and back Anterior (ventral) vs posterior (dorsal)
Transverse Into upper and lower Superior (above) vs inferior (below)

  • Proximal — closer to the trunk; distal — farther from the trunk (shoulder is proximal to the elbow; the elbow is distal to the shoulder).

🎙 You have to know your plane of reference — for the body it is the midline; when we get into the hand and the foot, the reference points for medial and lateral will be a little bit different.

2. Patient Positioning

  • Supine — lying on the back.

  • Prone — lying on the belly/stomach.

  • Side-lying — lying on the side; named for the side the patient lies on (right or left side-lying).

3. Applying the Terms

  • Sternum is anterior to the thoracic spine; the thoracic spine is posterior to the sternum.

  • Ankle: lateral malleolus outside, medial malleolus toward the midline.

  • Patella: suprapatellar (superior) vs infrapatellar (inferior) — terms describe the relationship, not the distance.

  • Radial vs ulnar clarify the forearm: the elbow's medial collateral ligament can be called the ulnar collateral ligament to distinguish it from the knee's MCL.

  • Hand: palmar (anterior in anatomical position) vs dorsal (posterior).

  • Foot: plantar = bottom of the foot; dorsal = the top/upper surface — the odd one out, explained later by embryological development of the lower extremity.

TOPIC 1.2
Movement Terms

1. Planes, Axes, and Their Motions

Plane Axis of rotation Motion direction Motions
Sagittal Medial–lateral Anterior–posterior Flexion, extension; plantarflexion, dorsiflexion
Coronal / Frontal Anterior–posterior Medial–lateral Abduction, adduction; ulnar/radial deviation; side bending; inversion, eversion
Transverse Superior–inferior (vertical) Rotational Internal/medial & external/lateral rotation; pronation, supination

🎙 Lecture demonstration: a tape dispenser spinning on its axis — orient the axis medial–lateral and the tape moves anterior–posterior; that is the sagittal plane.

2. Sagittal Plane — Flexion and Extension

Flexion Extension
Joint bends; angle between the bones decreases Joint straightens; angle increases
Shoulder: arm moves anteriorly Shoulder: arm moves posteriorly
Elbow: ulna–humerus angle decreases Elbow: ulna moves posteriorly, angle increases
Wrist: palm moves forward Wrist: dorsum of hand moves posteriorly
Hip: trunk–thigh angle decreases Hip: angle between trunk and thigh increases
Knee: lower leg moves posteriorly (angle still decreases) Knee: lower leg moves anteriorly
Ankle: plantarflexion — toward the sole Ankle: dorsiflexion — dorsum toward the tibia

★ The knee bends 'opposite' to other joints — flexion moves the lower leg posteriorly, but the joint angle still decreases. Reference the moving surface at the ankle: sole for plantarflexion, dorsum for dorsiflexion.

3. Coronal/Frontal Plane Motions

Abduction Adduction
Movement away from the midline Movement toward the midline ('adding it back together')
Shoulder or hip: limb moves out to the side Limb returns toward the body
Fingers: away from the third metacarpal (the hand's midline reference) Fingers: back toward the third digit

  • Ulnar deviation — hand moves toward the ulna; radial deviation — toward the radius.

  • Lateral flexion (side bending) — ear toward shoulder, or sliding a hand down the same-side thigh; named for the side you bend toward.

  • Inversion — plantar surface turns toward the midline; eversion — away from the midline.

4. Transverse Plane — Rotation

  • Cervical/trunk rotation: nose or shoulder turns right = right rotation; left = left rotation.

  • Shoulder and hip: external (lateral) rotation away from midline (foot turns out); internal (medial) rotation toward midline (toes turn in).

Pronation Supination
Medial rotation of the radius Lateral rotation of the radius
Palm faces posteriorly (anatomical position) or down (elbow flexed) Palm faces anteriorly (anatomical position) or up (elbow flexed)

5. Translatory and Combined Motions

  • Protrusion / retrusion — forward/backward slide (mandible at the TMJ).

  • Scapula: elevation (superior glide), depression (inferior glide), protraction (away from spine), retraction (toward spine).

  • Circumduction — combined flexion + abduction + extension + adduction (shoulder, hip).

  • Opposition — thumb contacts a finger; reposition — thumb separates from the digits.

6. Movement Analysis Practice

The lecture closes by naming every joint position in four photos — e.g., a 'triple flexed' limb (hip flexion + knee flexion + dorsiflexion) opposite a triple extended one; a supinated forearm identified because the subject looks at her palm. Practice describing whole-body positions joint by joint.

TOPIC 1.3
Peripheral Nervous System — Spinal Nerves

Learning Objectives

  • Identify and describe the structure, function, and location of nervous tissue.

  • Differentiate the components of a typical spinal nerve.

  • Relate neuromuscular deficits with the location of nervous-system pathology.

🎙 This recorded lecture bridges until Kenhub access opens — either version covers the same content.

1. Nervous System Overview

  • CNS = brain + spinal cord. PNS = spinal nerves (largest component) + cranial nerves (Module 10).

🎙 Transportation analogy: information packets (cars) travel between destinations (brain ↔︎ periphery) on an interstate (spinal cord) and side roads (spinal nerves).

2. The Bony Framework

  • Vertebral column: 33 bones, 5 regions — 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral (fused), 4 coccygeal (fused).

  • Vertebral foramen (one vertebra) → stacked = vertebral canal, housing the cord and roots.

  • Intervertebral foramen — the exit between adjacent vertebrae; formed by the articular processes, laminae, vertebral bodies, and the IVD.

3. Spinal Nerve Formation

The cord runs from the base of the skull to about L1–L2. Formation sequence: rootlets → roots → spinal nerve.

Anterior root Posterior root
Efferent / motor Afferent / sensory
Carries stimuli away from the CNS to peripheral targets Carries stimuli into the CNS from the periphery
Cell bodies in the anterior horn of the cord (no swelling) Cell bodies in the spinal (dorsal) root ganglion — the visible swelling

  • Anterior + posterior roots combine → spinal nerves are mixed nerves (both fiber types).

4. Branches of a Spinal Nerve

  • Posterior ramus — smaller; deep back muscles and skin of the back.

  • Anterior ramus — larger; most of the rest of the body at that segmental level.

  • Recurrent meningeal branches — re-enter the foramen; innervate the meninges and vertebral-canal structures.

White rami communicantes Gray rami communicantes
Arise from T1–L2 only Arise from all spinal cord levels
Carry preganglionic fibers Carry postganglionic fibers
Spinal cord → sympathetic chain ganglia Sympathetic chain ganglia → target

5. The 31 Pairs and How They Exit

Region Pairs Exit pattern
Cervical 8 C1 exits between skull and C1; C1–C7 exit above their vertebra; C8 exits below C7 (no C8 vertebra)
Thoracic 12 Below the corresponding vertebra
Lumbar 5 Below the corresponding vertebra
Sacral 5 Fused sacrum → S1–S4 exit via anterior & posterior sacral foramina; S5 via the sacral hiatus
Coccygeal 1 One nerve despite 3–5 segments; exits the sacral hiatus with S5

★ 8 cervical nerves but 7 cervical vertebrae — the above/below switch at C8 is classic exam material.

6. Spinal Cord Landmarks

  • Conus medullaris (medullary cone) — the end of the cord, ~L1–L2.

  • Cauda equina ('horse's tail') — spinal nerves continuing below the conus.

  • Enlargements = more wiring for the limbs: cervical, C5–T1 → upper limb (brachial plexus, future module); lumbosacral, L2–S3 → lower limb (lumbosacral plexus, next module).

TOPIC 1.5
Lumbar Vertebrae

1. The Vertebral Column

  • 33 vertebrae, 5 regions; height = bodies + IVDs. Motion happens mostly above the sacrum; sacral segments are fused and the coccyx fuses by ~age 30.

  • Bodies enlarge superior → inferior with increasing load; vertebral foramen shrinks in the same direction (fewer nerves remain in the canal — by the lumbar levels only the cauda equina).

  • Normal curves: cervical lordosis, thoracic kyphosis, lumbar lordosis (driven by the lumbosacral angle), sacral kyphosis.

2. Anatomy of a Lumbar Vertebra

  • Vertebral body — supports body weight.

  • Vertebral arch — protects the cord: pedicle (lateral border; short, thick) + lamina (posterior border; upper surface anchors the ligamentum flavum).

  • Seven processes — 4 articular (synovial joints with neighbors), 2 transverse + 1 spinous (muscle/ligament attachments).

⚕ Clinical Spotlight — Spondylolysis & Spondylolisthesis
Pars interarticularis — the lamina region between the superior and inferior articular processes.
Spondylolysis — fracture through the pars; the 'Scotty dog' fracture (the fracture line runs through the dog's neck on oblique view).
Bilateral fracture → the lamina no longer restrains the vertebra above → spondylolisthesis (e.g., L5 slides anteriorly on S1). Risk: neural compression.

3. Inside the Vertebral Body

  • Thin compact shell containing cancellous bone; the vertebral body bears about 50% of the axial load.

  • Trabeculae: vertical supports for the endplates; horizontal supports stiffen the vertical ones. Aging mainly reduces the horizontal trabeculae → endplate change.

  • Bone is dynamic: loss can start in the early 20s and shows as height loss in the bodies, not the discs. Compression + movement drive remodeling.

  • Consequences of poor loading/density: osteoporosis, traction spurs, osteophytes.

★ Compression and movement are the counter to age-related bone loss — a theme that returns throughout the program.

4. Intervertebral Foramen and Nerve-Root Naming

  • Formed by the notches of adjacent vertebrae + the IVD; passes the spinal nerve and its vessels.

  • Naming: the disc between L4 and L5 = L4–L5 disc; the root exiting that foramen is named for the vertebra above → the L4 root.

5. Zygapophysial (Facet) Joints

  • Superior articular process (below) + inferior articular process (above); synovial plane joints — slide only, with capsule and synovial fluid.

  • Innervated by two nerves (medial branches of the posterior rami, above and below) → a potential pain generator.

6. Sacrum, Coccyx, and Variations

  • Sacrum — 5 fused vertebrae; strength/stability for the pelvis; transmits body weight to the pelvic girdle. Males: larger S1 body; females: wider relative to length.

  • Landmarks: sacral canal (canal continuation), sacral hiatus (inferior opening), auricular surface (synovial SI joint), anterior & posterior sacral foramina.

  • Coccyx — 4 vertebrae fusing ~age 30; anchors pelvic floor muscles and part of glute max.

  • Variations (~5%): cervical rib at C7, sacralized L5, lumbarized S1, occasional lumbar rib at L1 — usually incidental.

7. Supply and Innervation

  • Arteries: segmental branches off the abdominal aorta — periosteal, equatorial, spinal branches. Venous drainage mirrors it via internal/external venous plexuses.

  • The column itself is innervated by the recurrent meningeal branches — the 'U-turn' branch re-entering the foramen.

TOPIC 1.6
Lumbar Ligaments and the Intervertebral Disc

1. Ligaments of the Lumbar Spine

Ligament Attachments Function
ALL (anterior longitudinal) Anterolateral vertebral bodies & IVDs; occiput/foramen magnum + anterior tubercle of C1 → upper sacrum Supports vertebral-body joints; prevents hyperextension. Thick and fibrous
PLL (posterior longitudinal) Posterior bodies (mainly the IVDs) within the canal; C2 → sacrum; continues intracranially as the tectorial membrane Narrower/weaker than ALL; resists hyperflexion; helps prevent posterior disc herniation
Ligamentum flavum Lamina to lamina (anterior surface above → posterior surface below) High elastin (yellow); resists lamina separation in flexion; aids return to erect posture
Interspinous Between spinous processes, base to apex; blends with flavum and supraspinous Resists flexion (prevents spinous separation)
Supraspinous Tips of spinous processes, C7 → sacrum; cord-like Resists hyperflexion; can make spinous processes harder to palpate when taut
Intertransverse Between transverse processes; sheet-like, blends with muscles Limits lateral flexion

★ Each ligament resists the motion that would stretch it: anterior ligaments check extension, posterior ligaments check flexion. Anterior → posterior order: ALL → body/IVD → PLL → (cord) → flavum → interspinous → supraspinous.

2. The Intervertebral Disc

  • Fibrocartilaginous joint between bodies; 1/3–1/4 of column length; 23 discs (6 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar); lumbar discs the thickest (~7–10 mm, ~4 cm across). Innervated via the sinuvertebral nerves.

Nucleus pulposus

  • Gelatinous proteoglycan core; more type II collagen — absorbs compression; conforms to pressure; avascular; sits more posteriorly in the lumbar spine; blends gradually into the annulus.

Annulus fibrosus

  • Outer ligamentous annulus — unites the vertebral rims; type I collagen resists tension; low proteoglycan; anterior layers thicker than posterior; only the outside fibers are innervated, by the sinuvertebral nerve.

  • Inner cartilaginous annulus — load-bearing; high proteoglycan.

  • Fiber layers alternate at 45° in opposite directions → overlapping tensile strength.

Cartilage endplate

  • Hyaline cartilage ~1 mm, caps the vertebral endplate; penetrated by small vascular buds; the diffusion pathway for disc nutrition and the barrier between avascular nucleus and vascular body.

Disc nutrition

  • The adult disc is avascular — everything moves by diffusion (nutrients in, waste out). Supply fails with: blood-supply changes, subchondral sclerosis, endplate ossification, lifestyle (e.g., nicotine narrows vessels) → inflammation, impaired turnover, cell death, a less healthy disc.

How the disc moves

  • The nucleus shifts toward the compressed side to absorb load; the outer annulus resists tension (traction), torsion, and separation on the convex side of a bend.

⚕ Clinical Spotlight — Disc Herniation
Annulus tears and nucleus material seeps out — a slow leak, not a jelly-donut pop — and it is treatable in physical therapy.
Most herniations are posterolateral: the nucleus sits posteriorly, the posterior annulus is thinner, and the PLL reinforces centrally but not where the nerves exit.
Herniated material can compress exiting nerves or the cauda equina → symptoms distal/remote to the level. Cord compression is emergent. Full management comes in the spinal course.

3. Curvatures and Motions

  • Normal: cervical lordosis, thoracic kyphosis, lumbar lordosis, sacral kyphosis. Scoliosis = abnormal frontal-plane curvature; alters weight bearing.

  • Lumbar motions: flexion/extension (sagittal), side bending R/L (frontal), rotation R/L (transverse).

Lumbar Palpation Skills List

Bony landmarks (posterior)

  • Lumbar spinous processes — palpate centrally; have the patient cross their arms and bend forward; feel each segment move under your fingers through flexion and extension.

  • Lumbar transverse processes — find the spinous process, move just lateral; one thumb on each side; patient crosses arms, then side bends and/or rotates; repeat each segment.

Soft tissue (posterior)

  • Erector spinae — patient prone; resist active trunk extension; palpate as a group along the column, lateral to the spinous processes on both sides.
