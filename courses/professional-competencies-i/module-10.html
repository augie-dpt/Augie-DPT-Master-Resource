TOPIC 10.1

Resilience and Grit

  • The module's framing scenario — "Alex," first-trimester PT student, 9 pm after a full lab day: anatomy midterm came back lower than expected, one more big quiz in two days, exhausted and discouraged. The options on the table: hope for the best, pull a late night, reach out to a peer or instructor, or re-evaluate the study strategies. Clinic will hand you the same structure constantly: difficult patients, setbacks, injuries that heal slowly, emotional fatigue, schedule pressure.
RESILIENCEGRIT
The ability to bounce back and recover from setbacks — and re-engagePassion + perseverance toward long-term goals (Duckworth) — the determination to keep pushing forward
Adapting under pressure: staying present under cognitive load, getting back on track after mistakes, handling daily uncertaintyHealthy grit includes: persistence · adaptability · intentional practice · long-term vision
In PT school it looks like: managing the emotional load of complex patients, meeting faculty and CI expectations without unravelingGrit is NOT: pushing through exhaustion, working without boundaries, or ignoring your wellbeing
  • These are professional competencies, not personality traits — they shape how you learn, how you care for patients, how you advocate, and whether you sustain a career. Duckworth's TED talk (6:12) is the required framing: grit isn't just working harder, it's a way of thinking that supports sustainable growth — passion held over years, not intensity held over a weekend.
  • The PositivePsychology.com article (5+ Ways to Develop a Growth Mindset Using Grit & Resilience) supplies the practice list: goal-setting, reframing failure, and social support — tools to start using this trimester, not concepts to admire.
  • The conclusion page adds the third leg: self-efficacy — the confidence to take on hard tasks and believe you can succeed. Resilience recovers, grit persists, self-efficacy fuels the attempt in the first place.

TOPIC 10.2

Growth Mindset

FIXED MINDSETGROWTH MINDSET
Intelligence and talent are innate and unchangeable — they can't be cultivated with effortAbilities develop through effort, perseverance, and learning (Carol Dweck's research)
Challenges and mistakes feel detrimental: "I can't do this" · blaming the CI, the patient, the situationChallenges, setbacks, failures, and mistakes are normal and expected — opportunities for learning and goal-writing
"The effort isn't worth it" — why put in the hours if I'm struggling?CI feedback becomes next week's goal; a setback becomes the thing to work on
"I avoid situations where I might look incompetent""Struggle means my brain is learning" · "feedback is data, not a verdict"
  • Why it matters in clinical education specifically: mistakes and feedback are built into the design — practice, error, feedback, repeat. A fixed mindset turns that loop into a threat; a growth mindset turns it into the curriculum. And once you're licensed, the constant mentor disappears — clinical education is your window to soak up feedback while it's abundant. Keep defensiveness and pride in check.

Developing a Growth Mindset — the 10 Strategies (Dr. Mierau)

  • The two umbrella moves: adopt a positive attitude toward learning and embrace challenges as opportunities for personal growth. All ten below serve one of those; find the three or four that speak to you.
StrategyThe working version
1. Embrace challengesEach obstacle you face and overcome builds self-efficacy — see it as opportunity, not obstruction
2. Believe in the power of effortThe process matters, not just the outcome; effort and hard work pay off even when the grade isn't the A you wanted — you're learning how to study and prepare
3. Cultivate a love for learningHealth care changes fast — what you learn now will be contradicted by future research. Curiosity is a career requirement, not a bonus
4. Emphasize the power of "yet""I can't do this" → "I can't do this YET." Skills develop over time; the word acknowledges it (her kids-on-bikes example — every new skill feels like the first bike ride)
5. Embrace failure as learningWHEN you make a mistake (not if): analyze it, adjust, and don't repeat it. Every faculty member can name the mistake they made as a student — and tell you they never made it again
6. Foster positive internal dialogueReplace "I'm never going to pass this clinical" with affirmations tied to yet. Cheesy, and it works
7. Seek constructive feedbackAsk peers, lab faculty, CIs constantly: how did that feel? did that make sense? Accepting feedback is itself a skill — and it feeds goal-writing
8. Surround yourself with positive influencesFixed-mindset, negative-self-talk company limits your growth; growth-minded peers turn each other's mistakes into shared learning — teaching each other is how you learn best
9. Set meaningful goalsRealistic, values-aligned, broken into small steps (SMART format), celebrated along the way. Goals you can meet every few weeks beat two giant year-long goals you fall short of daily
10. Stay persistentGrowth takes time; progress comes from consistent effort — long-term perspective through setbacks
🎙
For clinical education the deck compresses it to six verbs: VIEWING feedback as information · EXPECTING discomfort in complex patient interactions · BELIEVING clinical reasoning develops gradually · RECOGNIZING confidence comes after repeated practice · TRYING again after a poor first attempt · UNDERSTANDING that mistakes are data points, not incompetence.

MODULE 10

Sync Session — EQ, Resilience, & Grit in Action

  • Sync details are posted in your own Canvas module. The workshop's five goals: identify components of EQ/resilience/grit · recognize your internal reactions to stressors · apply EQ in clinical communication scenarios · construct a Behavior Chain for a stressful moment · create resilience-and-grit reframes for clinical growth.

1. EQ as a Clinical Superpower (bridging Module 9)

  • EQ = how well you understand yourself + how well you understand others. Clinical examples from the handout: self-awareness — noticing you're getting flustered during gait training; self-regulation — pausing before responding defensively to CI feedback; empathy — recognizing a patient's fear underneath their frustration; social skill — adapting tone for patients vs CIs vs family.
  • What's under difficult behavior: most challenging interactions stem from unmet emotional needs — to feel safe, understood, competent, respected, in control; needs for clarity, support, belonging. Listen for the need, not the tone.
LOW-EQ COMMUNICATIONHIGH-EQ COMMUNICATION
Reacts to the behavior · makes assumptionsResponds to the emotional need · uses curiosity, not judgment
Defends, controls, or withdrawsNames + validates the emotion
Rushed, sharp, closed-off tone and postureAdjusts tone, pacing, posture
Solves the task but damages trustStrengthens trust AND problem-solving
  • The High-EQ formula: NAME → VALIDATE → COLLABORATE. "It sounds like you're unsure…" → "That makes sense to me" → "Let's figure this out together — what would help you feel more comfortable?" Your regulation affects the patient's (and CI's, and peer's) regulation.

2. The Behavior Chain

LinkWorked example: CI gives abrupt feedback ("That transfer wasn't safe — be more efficient")
1. TriggerSomething happens externally — the CI's comment
2. ThoughtThe interpretation: "They think I'm incompetent"
3. EmotionEmbarrassed + anxious
4. Physical responseTight chest, flushed face, shallow breath
5. BehaviorQuiet withdrawal; stops asking questions
6. ConsequenceCommunication gap — CI assumes the student doesn't care or lacks confidence
7. Reframe"I can use this feedback to improve. Everyone learns efficiency with practice. I can clarify expectations and try again"
★
The point of the chain: see the pattern, interrupt it early (usually at the thought), and choose a more effective response — EQ names the emotion, resilience recovers, grit tries again. The handout's send-off is worth keeping: you will fail, you will struggle, you will grow — and that is how you become a great physical therapist.
