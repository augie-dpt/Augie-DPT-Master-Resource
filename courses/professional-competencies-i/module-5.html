TOPIC 5.1

Metacognition — Thinking About Your Thinking

  • The module's opening scenario: hours reviewing notes, walking into lab feeling prepared — and freezing when the patient scenario demands APPLICATION. Memorizing is not the same as thinking about your thinking. Metacognition = the ability to PLAN, MONITOR, and EVALUATE your own learning: recognize what you actually know, find the gaps, and change strategy when one isn't working. It's the bridge from rote memorization to clinical reasoning that sticks — and the same habit that makes a clinician who keeps learning for a career.
Ask yourself (the Canvas prompts — use them weekly)What it's really testing
When I study, do I think about HOW I'm learning, or just memorize?Whether you have a strategy at all
Do I know if I UNDERSTAND the material, or just recognize it?Recognition feels like knowledge until the practical exam
When a strategy isn't working, do I change it or keep grinding?Evaluation → adjustment is the whole loop
How often do I stop mid-study to check progress?Monitoring — the step almost everyone skips

TOPIC 5.2

Self-Assessment & Learning Strategies — Closing the Confidence Gap

  • Scenario two: you finish a practice practical CONFIDENT — then the score shows missed steps and overlooked details. That gap between perceived and actual performance is the topic (the TED-Ed video is the Dunning-Kruger effect in five minutes). The fix is evidence over feeling: self-testing, error analysis, teaching someone else, applying concepts to NEW cases, adjusting based on quiz/lab/instructor feedback. And when an assessment goes badly — analyze your preparation, don't blame the test.

📊 The LASSI (Learning and Study Strategies Inventory)

  • 10 scales, scored as percentiles: ANXiety · ATTitude · CONcentration · INformation Processing · MOTivation · Selecting Main Ideas · Self-Testing · Test STrategies · Time ManagemenT · Using Academic Resources
  • Take it online — the school number, username, and password are posted in your own Canvas module
  • The assignment: reflect honestly on your two or three lowest scales and pair EACH with a concrete strategy from McGuire Ch 5
  • The sync jigsaw: teams each dig into scales and teach the strategies back — arrive with your own report done

TOPIC 5.3

Time Management — Protecting the Hours That Matter

  • Scenario three: practical week, a nightly study plan, and then assignments + group meetings + life — exam day arrives with half the material reviewed. Time management here isn't tidiness; it's protecting deep learning, self-care, and clinical preparation. Tools: time audits (find where hours actually go), weekly scheduling (study time gets calendar slots exactly like class), prioritization when deadlines stack. The UBuffalo video opens with a calculation of how many study hours your credit load demands — run it for this trimester and compare it to your reality. Then defend sleep, exercise, and a personal life; they're part of the system, not the leftovers.

FROM THE READING

McGuire, Teach Yourself How to Learn — Ch 3, 5, 9 digested

Ch 3 — Metacognition

  • Metacognition (Flavell, 1976) = thinking about your own thinking — four abilities: think about your thinking · be consciously aware of yourself as a problem solver · monitor, plan, and control your mental processing · accurately judge your level of learning. The proof cases: Travis went 47, 52 → 82, 86 in intro psych after one 30-minute phone call; Dana went 80, 54 → 91, 97, 90 in physics. Not fiction — strategy.
  • The trap the chapter names: turning in work you're SURE is great, getting a D, doubting yourself, withdrawing, spiraling. If that's ever been you, the problem wasn't effort — it was that you couldn't accurately judge your own learning, and doing MORE of what you already do won't fix it. Different strategies will.

Ch 4–5 — The Study Cycle + the Ten Strategies (the engine Ch 5 runs)

The Study Cycle (LSU CAS)What each step is
1. PreviewSkim before class so your brain has the big picture — even 10 minutes right before lecture works
2. Attend classCollege lectures carry material the posted notes never will — plus the chance to ask
3. ReviewASAP after class: recall the lecture, fill note gaps — like watching a movie the second time
4. Intense study sessionsSet goals (2 min) → active learning 30–50 min → break 10–15 min → review 5 min. Typically 50–60 min total; 2–3 between classes + a couple at night = 4–5 hours of study without breaking a sweat
5. AssessDid the strategy work? Flashcards flopping? Switch to concept mapping. Adjust every cycle
  • The ten metacognitive strategies (Ch 5): previewing · preparing for active reading · paraphrasing · reading actively · using the textbook even when it's not required · going to class and taking notes by hand · doing homework without consulting solved examples · teaching the material to a real or imagined audience · working in pairs/groups · creating practice exams. You don't need all ten — but the reading and homework strategies are non-negotiable.

Ch 9 — Time Management, Test Taking, Stress

  • Time tips: semester-at-a-glance calendar first (big rocks in the bucket before sand) → weekly calendar with EVERYTHING scheduled, sleep included → "I have an appointment" for your protected study blocks (you don't owe Max the duck pond) → start homework the day it's assigned → prioritize by needs and wants, and forgive the mistakes. The commitment the book names: 20–25 study hours per week in intense-session format.
  • Test prep starts on day one, not the night before: build a test-prep schedule · find out exactly what's covered and teach it flawlessly to an audience · match practice to question TYPE (essay prep ≠ multiple-choice prep) · organize with charts/outlines · make your own practice exam.
  • Test day: dump formulas onto the page first · read directions carefully · survey and budget time · easiest questions first (confidence + priming) · expect memory blocks and move on — it comes back · deep breathing + positive self-talk.
  • After the test — exam wrappers: paper in thirds: how I prepared / what kinds of mistakes and why / what I'll do differently next time. Returned tests are the richest study document you own. And use the campus learning center — finding it is one search away.
