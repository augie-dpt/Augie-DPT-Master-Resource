TOPIC 7.1

Foundations of Professional Communication

  • Communication is the transfer of information — but in health care it's how trust is built, care is coordinated, and professionalism is expressed. Every interaction counts, from a hallway greeting to a formal evaluation note. It's also a therapeutic tool: how you listen and express yourself is part of the intervention.

1. Three Models of Communication

ModelHow it works
LinearOne-way: sender → message → receiver. Think a broadcast — no feedback loop. A message can be missed altogether and the sender never knows
InteractionalTwo-way channel: the receiver responds, sending feedback — but turns alternate, like walkie-talkies
TransactionalBoth parties send and receive simultaneously; the message is shaped by the context and the lived experience of each individual. This is the model that actually describes patient care

2. Six Components of Communication

ComponentWhat the lecture emphasizes
VerbalThoughtfulness · appropriate word choice · confidence · respect · attention to tone and cadence · awareness of your own nonverbals while speaking
ListeningOften overlooked and arguably the most underrated professional skill — see below
NonverbalBody language communicates interest or disinterest before you say a word — posture, eye contact, openness
WrittenEmails, notes, and everything else in professional and academic life: write clearly and proofread for clarity and professionalism
EnvironmentNoise, seating, privacy, time pressure — the room can enhance rapport or wreck it
ExperiencesEducation level, culture, and history shape how a message is interpreted — meet people where they are

3. Active Listening

  • Listening is how we gather information and gauge the situation — using the environment, the emotion, and the facts — and it's what produces shared understanding. Requirements from the deck: patience, good eye contact, and the discipline to NOT compose your response while the other person is still talking.
★
The BU Active Listening handout is required reading, and the 7.2 page adds a 15-minute TEDx talk (The Power of Listening). The exam-ready line: listening is a skill you practice, not a pause between your sentences.

TOPIC 7.2

Adapting Communication Across Contexts

  • Why it matters: better understanding → safer care → higher adherence → trust. Not every patient, peer, or colleague hears a message the same way — culture, health beliefs, stress, and environment all shape what's received.
  • The deck's quick framework before any important conversation: Person → Context → Purpose → Plan. Who am I talking to, in what setting, to accomplish what, and how will I confirm the message landed?

1. Adapting Across the Lifespan

AudienceStrategies from the deck
Children & adolescentsShort bursts · offer choices (autonomy) · show and do, don't just tell · watch caregiver cues · involve parents as partners
AdultsLink teaching to their roles and goals · be transparent about trade-offs · shared decisions
Older adultsAdult conversations (never elderspeak) · pace · large print and visuals · sensory checks (hearing, vision) · one single next step

2. Culture, Stress, and Learning Preferences

  • Culture & language (the CLAS spirit): humility · qualified interpreters for limited English proficiency (not family members) · integrate the patient's beliefs into the plan. The National CLAS Standards (HHS) are the required reading here.
  • Stress & cognitive load: a new diagnosis shrinks working memory. Name it, normalize it ("it's a lot to take in — that's normal"), then give one thing now and chunk-and-check the rest.
  • Learning preferences: say it · show it · do it · write it — the most effective teaching hits more than one channel, and the best communicators flex to the needs of the listener, not their own default.

3. The Three Core Tools

ToolHow to run it
Teach-BackAsk what they already know → tell the key points → ask them to explain it back in their own words. You're testing whether YOUR teaching worked — if they miss a step, re-teach it differently and check again
Ask–Tell–AskAsk (what do you know / what are your concerns?) → tell the key information → ask (reactions? next step?). Keeps the patient a participant instead of an audience
Plain languageHypertension → high blood pressure · biweekly → every other week · ambulate → walk · contraindicated → not safe for you · adhere → stick with your plan. Plain language is not dumbing it down — it's precision for the listener
  • Chunk-and-check: no more than ~3 chunks, a micro-summary after each, and a check before moving on. Understanding prevents overload.
🎙
The deck cites the AHRQ Health Literacy Universal Precautions Toolkit — universal means you use these tools with EVERY patient, not just the ones you guess need them. Guessing is how messages get missed.

TOPIC 7.3

Professional Dialogue & Feedback

  • Why this matters: communication failures read as professionalism flags (tone, timeliness, clarity), patient safety depends on clear communication (guarding, cueing, expectations), and strong dialogue now = clinical readiness later.

1. Two Feedback Frameworks

SBI (SITUATION–BEHAVIOR–IMPACT)PENDLETON'S RULES
Structure: "In [situation], I observed [behavior], which led to [impact]"Flow: learner says what went well → advisor reinforces → learner says what to improve → advisor adds specifics → agree on an action + follow-up
Add the inquiry: "What was going on for you?" — information first, judgment neverThe learner speaks FIRST at every step — builds self-assessment, not defensiveness
Best for quick, time-pressed moments: lab, Zoom, group projects, concise reviewsBest for lab debriefs, advising check-ins, and growth-oriented reflection

2. Worked Examples (from the deck)

ScenarioSBI + Pendleton follow-up
Group work"In our group project this week (S), your slides were uploaded after the deadline (B), which caused the team to work later that night (I). What barriers came up?" → "What could you do differently before the next deadline?"
Lab safety"During lab this morning (S), you arrived without proper footwear (B), which created a safety concern during spotting (I)." → "What will you do in next week's lab to make sure you're prepared for guarding?"
Peer communication"In the discussion forum (S), your response to a peer's post — 'that makes no sense' (B) — came across as dismissive (I)." → "How might you rephrase feedback so it encourages dialogue?"
  • Receiving feedback without defensiveness — Name, Normalize, Next step: name the topic ("Thanks for the note about citation practices") → normalize the impact ("I see how that affects integrity and clarity") → commit to a dated, specific next step ("I'll use the template on the next assignment and send a draft outline by Friday"). The instinct is to defend; the skill is to treat feedback as data, not a verdict.

3. Digital Professionalism

  • Netiquette = internet etiquette; cyberincivility = rude, dismissive, or unprofessional online behavior — and it damages academic and professional relationships just like the in-person kind. The deck's professionalism flags: emails with no greeting or subject ("Do we have class??" at 11:59 pm), chronic late uploads that push teammates into after-hours work, AI-style phrasing with no disclosure or references, and dismissive forum replies.
  • Required reading covers the Augustana + DPT conduct standards (professionalism and dress code, use of distributed materials, responsible technology use, email and social media policies) plus Degagne et al. (2020) on email in health professional education, and a set of how-to-email-a-professor guides. The through-line: email is professional writing — greeting, subject, clarity, proofread.

⚠️ Case connection — HIPAA and social media

  • A nurse at Texas Children's Hospital was fired for a HIPAA violation after posting about a rare measles case on Facebook — WITHOUT naming the patient.
  • Her profile listed her role and workplace, so the patient could be identified.
  • The lesson: digital professionalism and confidentiality extend to everything attached to your identity online. If your profile says where you work, your posts speak as a clinician.
  • Why it all matters: reliable micro-behaviors → safer labs and fewer near-misses; respectful, specific feedback → better group products and climate; the habits you form now → smoother clinical transitions and a patient-safety mindset (the deck cites TeamSTEPPS 3.0 — structured team training works because feedback skills are teachable and repeatable).
