TOPIC 9.1

Emotional Intelligence (EQ)

1. Why EQ Matters in Physical Therapy

  • PTs meet people at a moment of loss — of mobility, a pastime, the ability to work or provide; in pediatrics, parents facing uncertainty about their baby's future. You can't know the right thing to say in every instance, but curiosity plus empathy will always point you in the right direction (Dr. Fuentes). The Sophie's Neighborhood video is the module's living example: a child's rare condition, and what family-centered, emotionally intelligent care looks like around it.
  • We're drawn to people who are aware of their emotions and manage them well — and your future patients will value exactly those skills. The good news: EQ is a skill set, and skills can be practiced.

2. What EQ Is

  • Definition (Mayer & Salovey, 1993): emotional intelligence is a type of social intelligence involving the ability to monitor one's own and others' emotions, discriminate among them, and use the information to guide thinking and actions — chosen because it's the most pertinent to patient care: interventions are more meaningful when you can acknowledge both your emotions and your patient's.
Piece of the puzzleWhat it is
Cognitive intelligenceThe ability to acquire and apply knowledge and skills — relatively fixed
PersonalityThe combination of characteristics that form your distinctive character — relatively fixed
Emotional intelligenceSkills, behaviors, actions you can 100% change, improve, and grow — the growth-mindset piece
  • The four quadrants (from Emotional Intelligence 2.0, the course EQ book): the top half is about your own emotions — self-awareness and self-management; the bottom half is about others — social awareness and relationship management. PTs work in all four constantly: reflecting on personal growth, and interacting with colleagues, supervisors, and patients.
🎙
The Wittgenstein quote Dr. Fuentes shares via Brene Brown: the limits of my language mean the limits of my world. If you lack words for emotions, your interactions are limited too — adults need emotional vocabulary as much as the kids being told to use your words.

★
Dr. Fuentes' example of self-awareness under pressure: as a private-practice owner during the pandemic — visits collapsing, pivoting to telehealth — she leaned on watching herself, journaling, and getting to know herself under stress so her team didn't absorb it. Pick the strategies that name YOUR gaps, not the easy ones.

4. Three Emotions That Carry Clinical Practice

EmotionDefinition + why it matters
EmpathyA complex capability enabling individuals to understand and feel the emotional states of others, resulting in compassionate behavior (NIH). Builds therapeutic alliance and trust — and research supports it as a protective factor against burnout (the Therapy Insiders podcast goes deep here)
ResilienceThe capacity to withstand or recover quickly from difficulties; APA: successfully adapting to challenging life experiences through mental, emotional, and behavioral flexibility. Both/and: you need it AS a clinician, and you recognize and honor it IN patients
CompassionEmpathic understanding plus the desire to act on the person's behalf. For Sophie it looks like scheduling extra time to review X-ray findings, reading up on a new medication — or showing up at her nonprofit's fundraiser. It looks different in every situation
  • The lecture's closing summary — and the module's actual answer: stay curious, stay connected, and EQ grows.
